## ONE

They come in low over the water, and the water does something the mountain has never done.

Fish come out of it. Not one — a shoal of them, silver, out of the flat face of the sea and along beside the cloud for the length of a breath, and then back in.

Goshin is on his knees with his hands over the edge before his father can say anything, and Gohan is leaning out the other side, and neither of them has any idea what he is looking at, and both of them are laughing at it.

Then the sea is the first thing here that is not the mountain.

On the mountain, water is a thing you carry in a bucket, and it is a thing you can put down. Here it goes out past the edge of seeing. It moves without anybody moving it. Its sound is not a sound so much as a sound that keeps arriving.

Then there is a house on the water, and a noise coming out of the house that is not the sea.

The cloud comes down and his father steps off it as if off a step.

Goshin is off before it has stopped. He is at the sand, then in it, then on his back in it with his arms out, because the beach on an island is not the beach on a mountain and he did not know that, and now he does.

His brother comes off the cloud in the careful way, holding on until the last moment, and lands on the rock and not in the sand, which is his brother all over.

"Sand," Goshin says, to the sky.

"We live on a mountain," Gohan says.

"We live on the wrong mountain."

---

## TWO

The house is one room and the room is too small for the people in it, and nobody minds.

At the door Bulma is being told off for not coming sooner, and is not sorry, and has brought a box of manju which is opened before it is put down. A small old man takes one and says something that gets him called a dirty old man in a tone that suggests it is a Tuesday. A bald young man laughs at both of them.

"Where's Yamcha?"

Bulma stops chewing. "I didn't tell him," she says. "I found out everybody was getting together today, and I *didn't tell him.*"

"He'll be hurt."

"He'll be hurt," she agrees, and takes another one.

"Where's Launch?"

"Five years ago she went off after Tien," the bald young man says. "Nobody's seen her since."

Outside, the cloud comes down, late, and his father steps off it as if off a step, with a leaf in his hair.

And there is a turtle.

Goshin crouches in front of it and looks at it, and the turtle looks at him.

"Are you from the mountain?"

The turtle does not say anything for a while. Goshin waits, which is the one thing about him nobody has ever thought to praise.

"I am from the sea," the turtle says at last, slowly, and kindly.

"I'm from the mountain."

"I know."

Goshin sits down on the floor beside it and puts one hand flat on the shell, and the shell is warm, and the turtle lets him.

"Yahhoo!" his father says, from the doorway. Then, "Oh— whose kid is this?"

The room stops.

"He's mine," his father says.

"Yours."

"Both of them."

Bulma puts her manju down and looks, properly, at the two of them, and then laughs — a real laugh, surprised out of her. "Two," she says. "You had two of them, and nobody thought to write."

"Gohan," the small old man says, and comes and puts his face close. "Son Gohan. You gave him your grandfather's name."

"Grandpa's name," his father says, pleased.

"And how old is he?"

"Four," Gohan says, and then remembers, and says it again properly: "Four years old."

"Polite," Bulma says, wondering. "A child of *his*, and polite."

"Their mother's got opinions," his father says, and everybody decides not to ask what the opinions are.

---

## THREE

Roshi thinks he is being clever about it, and the boy answers the question he was actually asked.

"And what will you be, when you're big?"

"A great scholar," Gohan says at once, the way a thing said to him many times comes out.

"A scholar." Roshi looks at the father. "Not a fighter."

"Ask their mother."

"Uh — Goku." The bald young man has been looking at the boy the way you look at a thing you cannot place. "His tail."

His father looks. "Huh? Oh. Yeah. Same as me, before."

Both of them. There are two tails in that room, and one belongs to Goshin on the floor with his hand flat on a shell, and it has been there the whole time.

The bald young man is not comforted. "Look — nothing *strange* ever happens with him? Nothing ever changes?"

"Changes."

"Like. For instance." He is choosing his words with both hands. "On the night of a full moon. Anything?"

"Oh, a full moon." His father thinks about it. "Dunno. We go to bed early, all of us."

Roshi's eyebrows go up, and then come down, and then he says, quickly, "Nothing. Nothing at all. That's fine. That's good."

"It's not a stupid question," the bald young man says, to nobody.

"Is he strong?" he says then. "Like you were?"

"He's got a lot in him. I said I'd train him up a bit, and their mother went through the roof."

"Why?"

"She reckons the world's peaceful now, so there's no need for it. It's books now, she says. It's the age of studying."

"And you agreed to that," Bulma says.

"I've got a lot of time to agree to things in," his father says.

It is the hat.

"Hey. That on his hat. Is that a Dragon Ball?"

"That's the Four-Star," his father says. "It's my grandpa's. I hunted it out again and put it on there for him." Then, as an afterthought, the way he says everything: "Found the Three-Star and the Six-Star the same time. They're at the house."

Bulma looks at him with her mouth open. "You've got two Dragon Balls in your house."

"Yeah."

"Just — *in the house.*"

"In the house," his father says.

She sits back and something crosses her face that is not entirely a joke. "So if you got all seven," she says, "would you wish for something?"

"Don't reckon I've got a wish," his father says. "Never did."

---

## FOUR

His father stops laughing.

It happens the way a sound stops. One moment there is a laugh, and then he is standing and not laughing, with his head turned a little, the way you turn your head to listen for something that is not in the room.

"What?"

"Something's coming."

Bulma sits up straight. "Coming? Is it Yamcha?"

"It's not Yamcha."

The bald young man has gone quiet in a way he does not go quiet. "What is it?"

"Big."

Then it is properly quiet, and out on the rock a bird that was standing there leaves.

The sea keeps arriving. The small old man's fan stops moving. At the back of the room a fly is at the window, and on an island where nothing else is happening, the fly is the loudest thing in the world.

Then the sky does something a sky should not do.

It gets a seam in it.

The seam comes down and runs out across the water away from the island, like something was dropped in the sea a long way off and the surface took its time saying so.

Then the air claps, once.

The sand jumps. Goshin is already on his feet with his hands up.

There is a man in the air over the beach.

---

## FIVE

"Kakarot."

Raditz says it the way a man says a name he has waited years to use.

"Kakarot. You've grown. I knew you at once — you look just like our father."

"…Kakarot?"

"What is this place? You were sent here to wipe out its people. What have you been *playing* at?"

The bald young man comes out onto the beach with his hands up, friendly, the way you talk to a man who has had too much to drink.

"Now then. I don't know who you are, but off you go. Off you go. You can't be drunk in the middle of the day, come on—"

"Krillin. Don't get near him."

"Oh— *aaah—*"

The tail comes round.

---

## SIX

He goes through the wall.

Wood comes apart the way wood does and stays apart, and afterwards a piece of it comes away and falls inside the house, and the turtle says, "Oh," very quietly.

Bulma's arms come round both boys at once. "Don't move," she says, and does not move either.

"…A tail," the bald young man says, from the sand, in a voice that has gone thin. "*He's* got a tail."

Raditz turns round, unhurried, and lets them all look at it.

"Now you know what I am," he says. "And you. Do you remember anything of it? A shock. To the head. When you were small."

"…A shock."

"In the head. As a child."

"Yeah." His father puts a hand up and touches the back of his own head, which none of them has ever seen him do. "There's a scar there. I don't remember getting it."

"There," Raditz says. "That would do it."

The small old man has come forward, and his voice is careful. "He told me this," he says. "*Your* grandfather told me. He found something in a valley — a round pod, and inside it a baby with a tail. He said the child was wild and would not be tamed. And then one day the child fell and struck his head and nearly died of it. After that he was a different boy. A good one."

"So that's me," his father says. "That's what I was."

Then Raditz says the rest of it in the flat voice of a man reading out a form.

A planet with a name, out in the dark, that a whole people came from, and that is not there any more — *an asteroid*, he says, the way you would mention weather. Our father and mother too, he says. Four left. Four, out of everything.

"You are not from this planet," he says. "You are a Saiyan. And I am your brother."

"Say something," Bulma says, to the father. "If he's an alien, what's he doing here?"

"He was sent to clear it," Raditz says. "And instead he—"

"What I'm doing here," his father says, "is standing in the way of a man I've never met."

---

## EIGHT

"There is a world," Raditz says. "Good land. Worth a great deal. Three of us would struggle with it. Four would not. You are still my brother's blood. And after that, this planet—"

"No."

"Think. When the moon draws full and the shape comes on you — do you suppose that is a *curse*? That is what we are. It is what we are *for*."

The sand is quiet.

"The moon?" the bald young man says, from the hole in the wall.

His father does not turn round. "That's for me, then, is it. Round moon, and something happens to me."

"It is what we are for."

"Not to me it isn't." His father says it flatly, and it takes a moment for the words to arrive. "I haven't got the thing that does it. It came off years ago."

"It came *off.*" Raditz's voice does not rise, which makes it worse. "You cut off your tail. You had a birthright and you *misplaced* it, and now you stand there with a house and a woman and—" he looks at the two of them, and the look takes its time, "—and these."

His father is quiet for a second. Then he says, "I don't care what planet I came out of, or that you're my brother. Krillin's right. People like you are the lowest." He puts his feet apart on the sand. "I'm Son Goku. I grew up down here. Get off my island."

"You're scared," Raditz says. "That's what that is. Come with us and be a Saiyan again."

"I'd rather die than lend you a hand," his father says. "And I mean that."

---

## SEVEN

The instrument speaks.

It has been speaking the whole time, in the glass over his ear, which is how he found this island at all — a reading, and then a reading further out, and then a smaller reading standing in the sun in front of a woman. Now the glass fills with a noise like a fly in a jar.

Raditz's head comes round. He looks at Gohan's tail, and then at Gohan. Then at Goshin, standing in front of Bulma with his hands in fists at his sides.

The glass makes its noise again.

"Four hundred," Raditz says.

Nobody says anything.

"Four hundred." He says it twice the way a man checks a weight he has already checked. "At four years of age. Untrained. With nothing done to it."

He crouches. He does it the way a man does a thing he has not done in a long time and finds he still can.

"What is this one called," he says.

"Goshin," his father says, from the sand. "His name's Goshin."

Raditz looks at him. Then at the boy.

"Goshin," he says, and it is not the same word in his mouth — it is a word he has decided is a mistake. "Kakarot's son has no need of a name some village gave him."

"His name—"

"His name," Raditz says, "is Broc."

And the island is quiet, and the sea goes on doing what the sea does underneath, and the boy hears a word he has never heard used for himself, spoken in the voice of a man who is not asking.

Nobody says otherwise.

Nobody in that house says otherwise, and there is no one on that island who has the standing to.

---

## NINE

It happens quickly, and then it takes a long time.

His father moves first — before anything, with no noise at all — and then Raditz's knee is in his stomach.

There is a sound like a door slamming in another room. His father's feet come off the ground and he goes down and the sand takes him, and he does not get up.

"Goku—!"

"Stop—!"

Raditz is already turning. Bulma gets both boys behind her, and Raditz reaches past her the way a man reaches past a curtain.

Goshin's feet are in the air a moment later.

Raditz holds both of them, one under each arm, like a man carrying two sacks that are not heavy.

"A hundred of them," he says, to the man in the sand. "By this time tomorrow. Pile them here, where I can see them. Then we'll discuss how many sons you have."

Raditz looks at the two of them, one under each arm, and something in his voice is almost reasonable.

"I'd rather not have to kill my brother's child," he says. "So don't make me."

"Put them—"

The sand jumps.

Raditz is standing in the air over the beach.

Krillin is pulling himself out of the hole in the wall. Roshi has both hands on the ground. Bulma runs down the beach and does not stop at the water and goes in to her knees with her arms up.

The island gets smaller.

The beach. The house with the hole in it. The people.

It all goes small together.

His brother is saying something. The wind takes most of it.

"Goshin."

"What."

"Hold on," Gohan says.

So he does. His hands are on an arm that is not his father's, and the wind is taking his hair, and his brother has told him to hold on, and it is not help either, but it is the same word.

The island is a stone in the sea, and then it is a fleck, and then the sea is the whole world and there is nothing in it at all.

---

## TEN — CUT HOME

A yard on a mountain, in the last of the light.

The shadow of the house has come off the yard and gone up the slope, the way it does in the evening.

There is food out on the step. Not much, and set out properly: three bowls, and one of them bigger than the others.

A woman stands in the middle of the yard with her sleeves tied back and her hands still, and looks down the road that goes off the mountain.

The birds have gone quiet.

She waits the way people wait when they have already decided to be angry and have not yet had anybody to be angry at.

A chicken comes past the step, stops, and looks at the three bowls and thinks about it.

"No," she says, without turning round.

The chicken goes away.

Twice she walks to the end of the yard and looks down the road, and twice she comes back.

The rice is done. She can hear it not doing anything any more, and she does not go in to it.

The light goes off the yard.

She does not go inside.
