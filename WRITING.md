# WRITING — how a chapter reads, and how it is shot

**Owns: the prose law and the camera.**

---

## 1 · The mix

Anime mass, manga economy. **Balanced, clean, readable** — under-styling fails and over-styling fails harder. **Readability outranks effect.** If a device is noticeable, the device has failed.

- **Anime gives:** time to sit in a room, weather that is doing something, a reaction held two seconds too long.
- **Manga gives:** the cut that arrives before you are ready, a page with one line on it, no transition at all.

---

## 2 · The band (arithmetic, not taste)

| | |
|---|---|
| length | **4,400–6,600** words for the default anime-episode form; accepted legacy scene chapters use their original band |
| average sentence | **8 – 14** words |
| longest sentence | **60** words — a hard cap |
| dialogue | **42 – 70%** of prose lines — the gate's floor. **Aim for 45+**; below that a chapter reads like reportage |

---

## 3 · Sentences

1. **One idea per line.** A line that does two things is two lines.
2. **Verbs first.** He does not "become aware of"; he looks.
3. **Concrete nouns only.** A thing you could photograph or put on a table.
4. **No stage directions for feelings.** "He was sad" is a note to an actor.
5. **The last line of a scene is short.** Every time.
6. **Never explain the rule the scene just obeyed.**

---

## 4 · The camera — shot budgets in words

| Shot | Words | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **pan** | 30–80 | the place, before anyone in it matters |
| **ritual** | 40–120 | a task done properly. The house's whole texture lives here |
| **stare-down** | 15–60 | two people, and the thing neither says |
| **clash** | 10–25 | two bodies, one exchange |
| **impact** | 2–6 | the frame that lands. No adjectives |
| **smear** | 3–8 | a brief movement with a verb and a direction |
| **reaction** | 3–10 | *someone other than the person who caused it* |

**A scene must name its shots, and the lengths must be uneven.** A scene of equal-length beats is a list.

---

## 5 · Devices, and the trap in each

| Device | Use | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| **the hold** | a paragraph where nothing happens and everything does | held too long it is boredom |
| **the reaction** | the cost, seen on a face that is not fighting | never on the actor of the action |
| **the world cut** | two lines away from the fight, to show its size | more than two lines is a subplot |
| **burst grammar** | short clauses into impact, one long line after | the long line must carry the aftermath, not the hit |
| **recovered silence** | sound returning in layers | once per chapter |
| **withheld frame** | cut before the thing is finished | the reader must know what was withheld |
| **the ritual** | hands doing a job correctly | never decorative — it must cost time |
| **cut home** | canon's own device: away from the event, to where someone is waiting | earns its place only if the wait is unanswerable |

---

## 6 · Before you write a scene, three questions

1. **What does he want in this scene that he cannot say?**
2. **Who reacts, and is it someone who did not cause it?**
3. **What is withheld, and from whom?**

No answers, no scene.

---

## 7 · Do not re-teach canon

**The cloud carries only the pure of heart.** Canon said so in *Dragon Ball*, at length, with jokes (C115) — so this serial **records** it (`STATUS §4`, `O023`) and **never stages it**. A scene that puts him on the Nimbus to show it is a scene the reader has already seen in a series that came first.

**The test for any such beat:** *has canon already made this point on screen?* If yes, it belongs on `STATUS` as a line with a witness, and the chapter's job is to **use** the fact, not prove it. The cloud rule remains background. Do not introduce a new cloud scene to demonstrate or re-prove it.

---

## 8 · Drawing a family resemblance

**Never a percentage.** "He is 99% his father" is a casting note from a producer, not a line of prose. A reader cannot see a fraction. **Draw what a camera sees:** the jaw, the hair that will not lie down, the way he stands with the weight on the same leg every time — and then the one thing that is his mother's, which is his eyes.

**The half-second is a shooting instruction.** When a scene needs him to think and the rule forbids interiority, give his face the lag: the room reacts, and *then* he does. Nothing is stated and everything is shown.

**One design line per chapter.** Seven sections of `STATUS` is a lifetime of work, not a paragraph's. A chapter shows **one** — the hands behind the back, the downhill side of the path, the refusal to eat what he did not watch being made — and says nothing about the rest.

## 9 · Forms, and how he never announces one

**He does not say the name of a transformation out loud, and neither does the narrator.** Super Saiyan 1, 2 and 3 are **finished** — perfected, internal, part of the engine. So there is **no hair-colour beat, no lightning, no counting, no "this is to go further."** The power is simply present, and the page proves it by what he breaks.

**What a scene may show instead:** the fidgeting stopping (`STATUS §1`), the room going quiet, an opponent's confidence arriving a second too early.

**Rage is cold and is never a scream.** **Super Saiyan 4 is earned at the ape** — write the fear before the fur, every time.

**And the gate is still a gate:** no god ki until the crossing, which is written **once, late, and one-directional** (`STATUS §12`). After it, god is his **floor** — never a power-up, never a switch. **Never write Blue.**

**The god rungs are a confession, not a fireworks display.** God 1, God 2, God 3 **leak** — write what being sensed costs him, never the glow. And **God 4 is silent**: when it arrives, the loudest thing in the scene should be everybody else realising they cannot feel him.

**A conversion, never a swap.** When he reaches for a rung, write the **reach** and then the wrongness of the result: the hair that does not come, the gold that is not there, the thing the enemy was waiting for failing to arrive. **His transformations are a mistranslation everyone else can hear — and the expectation is the weapon.**

**A god who is still a person.** After the crossing, **nothing is subtracted** (`STATUS §14`). He gets hungry, he laughs at the wrong moment, he sulks, he is wrong about people, he cries at the same things he cried at before. **Serenity is the failure mode of writing a god** — if a scene makes him calm, distant or above a joke, it is the wrong scene. The test: *could this man still be recognised by his mother?* If not, cut it.

**The era lock is the first thing to check in any scene.** At four he is a boy: hungry, polite, heavy for his age, one tail, no golden transformation or god ki; ordinary ki may be visible. **He flies, uses techniques, learns visibly and grows; he is never less capable than Gohan under the standing authored rule.** The ladder is a ceiling on file so that no future scene contradicts it — **it is not a costume to dress him in now.** If a chapter reaches above the first rung of `STATUS §13`, the chapter is wrong.


## §10 · The episode grammar — how a chapter is built like an anime

**A scene is not an episode.** A chapter that runs to 3,000 words in one room, with one lens and one day, is a *scene*, and it will read as a chapter of a novel rather than as twenty-two minutes of a broadcast. **Episode form is the default for new chapters**, 4,400–6,600 words, using the grammar below. The first two legacy chapters remain scene-length, including their explicitly authorized targeted revisions; a complete episode-form rebuild has not been authorized by this revision. Do not default a new chapter to a single-location scene.

**The eleven devices. A chapter that claims episode form carries at least seven.**

1. **Cold open.** One image before the title, no dialogue, and the world already running. Canon opens on weather and landscape because it is cheap and it is true.
2. **The narrator, who is a voice and not a mind.** He states time, place, and what is *not* happening. He may report events in other places — 「一方 そのころ…」 — **without the camera going there.** This is how the anime crosses the world without breaking a room.
3. **The title card.** After the cold open, and its own beat of silence. Ours goes in the chapter's own words: **EIGHT HOURS.**
4. **The countdown.** Use a clock only when it fits the actual sequence and sourcing; never invent a delay to preserve the device. In the approved Chapter Three revision, *Eight Hours* is the title and Chi-Chi's schooling reference, not a literal countdown. Other episodes may use a sourced countdown where appropriate.
5. **Hard cuts.** Not transitions. A blank line and a new image at a different distance or a different subject: the yard, then the road, then the window, then the gate. **Never explain the cut.**
6. **The insert.** A hand, a dial, a page, a peg. The anime's cheapest and best trick: it tells you what a person will not say.
7. **The held frame.** One wide shot, longer than comfortable, with nothing in it but what is happening. Somebody standing at a gate. Two of them, small, from a distance.
8. **Sound before sight.** An engine before the truck, a voice before the man, a knock before the door. Canon does this relentlessly.
9. **The eye-catch.** A still frame at the middle with the programme's own voice over it, and then the same frame resumed. Ours: *"This programme continues after a message."*
10. **The loop-back.** A shot repeated at the end that opened the chapter, and the difference is the point.
11. **The preview.** The anime's closing device, and the most useful thing in this file: **it lets a chapter say what it did not show.** Images or a narrator's flat promise and a title in quotes; honor any narrower camera restriction, as Chapter Three does by keeping its preview on the house and road. **It is also a promise to the reader that the rest of the world exists**, which no scene-form chapter can make.

**Keep the grammar readable.** Narrator, title cards, eye-catches and previews may be signposted as in Chapter Three; do not clutter the story with technical timecodes or a shot list. Use bold countdowns, hard cuts and held frames when they serve the episode.

**And the POV law survives it.** Camera movement is not interiority. **The narrator may say that a man is running down a road a million kilometres long; he may not tell you what the man is thinking.** Cutting is not entering. `STATUS §1` and the no-interiority rule hold at any frame size.

### Sequence and coverage gate

The user directs: **do not miss things next time**. Before drafting an episode, read it in full and make an ordered inventory of its scenes and identifiable beats, including conversations, fight exchanges, reactions and cutaways—not just the climax. Check each against the finished chapter: dramatized on the page; already dramatized in an accepted overlapping scene; altered by a stated, consequential divergence; or explicitly deferred to the immediately following chapter. Do not replace intervening events with a recap or silently skip an independent thread.

Start from the actual end of the preceding chapter. If the episode will not fit the chapter band without cutting events, continue it in another sequential chapter rather than omit them. Goshin's presence can change what happens; show how, rather than forcing the untouched canon outcome. The scene inventory and its coverage check are research records, not a substitute for the story.

Captions establish spoken material, not every silent frame. Use additional visual evidence when available and mark any unresolved visual details as unverified or authored; a complete caption read is not a claim to have watched or verified every image.

## §11 · How to write a fight

A fight must be followable in one pass. Establish who is present, where they are, what they are trying to do and what stands in the way. The reader should know who acts, where the action goes, what makes contact and what changes.

- **Geography:** establish relevant ground, boundaries, cover and bystanders before they matter. Give each movement a destination.
- **Sequence:** put the cause, action, impact and response in a clear order. Use names when a pronoun could mean either twin.
- **Capability:** show Goshin flying, using or learning techniques when the scene calls for it. Gohan acts and grows too. Their relative standing is an authored rule, not a fixed scouter number. For the current Kame House abduction, the user rules that Raditz cannot take Goshin conscious: his power rises in anger and he must be knocked out first (`O080`). Once he wakes, write the consequences of his capabilities, not a passive re-capture. This encounter-specific rule is not an invented general battle-power formula.
- **Scale:** choose effects from demonstrated feats, the chosen attack, aim, condition, control and surroundings (`§16`). Follow the damage through to its consequences.
- **Dialogue:** let an authority give a clear order and a character act. Do not have a character announce a fact already visible. Named canon techniques and appropriately declared invented techniques may be used; a scouter can print a sourced or authored number when its presence makes sense.
- **Speed:** describe displacement, contact and aftermath. Never tell the reader that nobody could see the action, compare a supernatural effect to ordinary furniture, or interrupt to say it is impossible.
- **Adaptation:** seeing a technique can help Goshin learn it; training and resistance have to be shown where relevant. In deliberate training he masters or perfects the abilities he already has before pursuing new ones, and gives the foundations serious attention (`O104`, `O105`). The user allows instinctive copying during emergencies. Show ordinary practice and eventual mastery, not a parade of new moves or a claim that nothing he knows is ever finished. A temporary defeat or exhausted landing does not erase retained skill.

A fight may contain many supernatural actions. It does not require a predetermined number of blows, an onlooker cut, an already-stated twist, a mandatory charge or a specifically small aftermath. The plain-words crib in `CRAFT.md` is a test of clarity, not prose that must be repeated in a chapter.

## §12 · How to write the supernatural

Treat flight, ki, transformation and impact as events in the scene, not as riddles. Name the actor when needed, show the movement and make the result clear. The writing may describe light, force, sound, injury and the landscape directly; it need not hide an attack's source or name.

Do not compare supernatural action to ordinary furniture or pause to explain that it is impossible. Do not report that an event could not be seen. A reader should understand the action from its cause and consequence. Sound can arrive later where distance makes that appropriate, but there is no mandatory delay and no limit of one supernatural event per scene.

**Era:** at four Goshin is mortal and has ordinary ki, flight and techniques. No divine ki or early divine transformation. Energy effects can be visible; their existence does not make him a god. The separate future rules stay locked until the story reaches them.

## §13 · Name them

**A name is not a reward for reaching the end of a paragraph.** Once a character has been named on the page, the prose calls them by that name — every time. **No "the taller boy", no "the small one", no "the demon", no "the woman with blue hair".**

**The three legal exceptions, and there are only three.**

1. **Before the name is known.** A stranger may be "the man at the gate" until he is introduced, and the moment of the name is a deliberate beat. **Use it once, then never again.**
2. **For a character who is never named at all.** The teacher on the bicycle, the man with the hoe, the shopkeeper, the driver. **They stay descriptions, because they are furniture with faces** — and that is a choice, not an accident.
3. **Inside dialogue.** People call each other "the old man" and "your grandfather" and "boy" out loud, and they will keep doing it. **Quotation marks are a licence. The narration is not.**

**Why this rule and not a matter of taste.** An epithet tells the reader *how to see* a person instead of who they are — it puts the camera between the reader and the character, which is exactly what a no-interiority book cannot afford. **It also leaks information the narrator should not have.** "The smaller one" is a judgement; "Goshin" is a fact. And in a serial with two four-year-old sons, **"the taller boy" is not a style, it is a puzzle** — the reader stops to sort them out, and the scene is gone.

**The tell that you are doing it wrong:** if a character's epithet changes as the scene changes — "the tall man", then "the man with the scar", then "the scarred man" — **the prose is auditioning synonyms for a name it already knows.** Cut all of them.

**Gated.** `check_names` fails any chapter or specimen that uses a descriptive epithet in narration for a character the same file names. If the epithet is a real choice, the character must stay unnamed — in which case declare it in the footer as furniture.

## §14 · No riddles — the law for invented material

**Canon is easy to follow because the audience already knows the story. Invented material has no such help, and it fails in a specific way: it makes the reader decode.** A teacher who speaks in proverbs, a boy who announces a fact as if it were a mystery, a line that sounds wise and means something the reader has to guess at. **That is what a riddle is, and it is a bug, not a style.**

**The laws.**

1. **Everybody says what they mean.** A character may be rude, blunt, contemptuous, or silent. **A character may not speak in aphorisms.** *"If you can't beat a thing, beat the thing it needs"* is banned permanently. **Canon's teachers say: "Grab his tail."**
2. **State the want and the stake in the first three lines of any invented scene.** Who wants what, and what happens if they do not get it. If a scene has to be explained afterwards, the explanation belongs at the front.
3. **Make the aim and consequence clear without repeating what the reader has just seen.** If an instruction is needed, the person with authority gives it; the learner acts. A character may explain unshown information they actually know, but must not announce an already-visible fact merely to supply a verdict.
4. **No revelation that is only a revelation to the reader.** Gohan noticing a tail is not a twist; it is an already-visible fact; let the authority order a tail grip and show the action instead.
5. **A question asked in the scene gets answered in the scene.** If a character asks "what do you mean?", the answer arrives within a few lines. **Canon does exactly this** — Goku asks, and somebody explains, at length, twice.
6. **The stranger test.** Read the invented paragraph to somebody who has not read the chapter. **If they cannot say what happened in one plain sentence, the paragraph is wrong — even if it sounds good.** Good-sounding and unclear is the exact failure mode of original prose.

**What this does not ban.** Silence, contempt, jokes, a character refusing to explain, a mystery the *characters* also cannot solve. **A character being mysterious is fine. The writing being mysterious is not.**

**A named situation before the scene.** Every specimen opens with **WHAT THIS IS**, one sentence, no style (`O053`). Same for any invented chapter beat that is not obvious from canon. **Name the situation, then write it.**

**Not machine-checkable, and said plainly.** No script can tell an aphorism from a line of dialogue. **This law is kept by the stranger test and by the reader**, and the reader saying "I don't understand this" is the check failing, not the reader.

## §15 · The flow — how the impossible is allowed to happen

**Three rules and a shape.** The rules are about what not to do; the shape is what is left when you stop doing them.

**Rule one — never tell the reader they could not see it.** *"In the blink of an eye", "faster than the eye could follow", "nobody saw him move", "so fast it was invisible"* — **all one move, and it is the weakest move available.** It describes an absence instead of an event, and it asks the reader to be impressed on the narrator's instruction. **Write the displacement instead:** he is at one stone, then he is at the other, **and the ground says what happened** — the dust rings, the feet in it to the ankle, the boy on his back four paces past his stone. **The reader does the arithmetic and feels the speed; the narrator does not report that it was fast.**

**Rule two — do not compare the impossible to something ordinary.** *Like a door closing · like pages turning · like water out of a tipped bowl · like sand out of a fist.* **Each of those cuts the event down to furniture**, and the moment a boy's strength leaving him is "like a bowl tipped", the reader is looking at a bowl. **The impossible gets described in its own physics: weight, pressure, heat, light, silence, and the state of the ground afterwards.** Save the ordinary comparisons for the ordinary — a boy's breathing, a chicken, a telephone.

**Rule three — do not stop to explain that it is strange.** The reader knows. **One sentence of event, then the next sentence of event.** No narrator's aside, no "and that should not have been possible". **Flow is the absence of commentary.**

**The shape, and it is the same shape every time:**

> **cause you can point at → the event → the consequence left in the world.**

Goshin puts his hand on Gohan's chest and pushes. Gohan is on his back. The dust is still in the air. **Three sentences, no gaps, and nothing the reader has to fill in.** If a step is missing, the reader supplies a wrong one — and that is what "I don't understand" actually means.

**And do not let a character announce what the scene already shows.** A boy saying *"he has a tail"* when the reader has been looking at the tail for two chapters is **a revelation to nobody**; the line reads as a riddle because it has no work to do. **Let the person with authority give the order** — *"His tail. Take it. It is the only part of him that is not strong."* — **and let the boy do it.**

## §16 · Fight scale — feats, context and consequences

**No fixed conversion:** a printed battle-power reading does not mechanically determine collateral, and it does not guarantee a fight's winner. A reading can change with suppression, condition or a concentrated attack. Do not assign a moon feat to Piccolo's earlier 408 reading or Nappa's city attack a measured 4,000: those values were obtained separately (`C130`, `C141`).

Before the fight, establish:

1. **Participants and location:** where each person is, what they want and who is at risk.
2. **Ability and condition:** relevant demonstrated feats, age, training, fatigue, injury and control. Include a reading only when sourced or openly authored for that moment.
3. **Action:** the attack, target, aim and surroundings. A body strike may be devastating or local depending on context; not all large feats require a lengthy charge.
4. **Aftermath:** injury, terrain, bystanders and resources in the causal chain. A deliberate small spar can leave a trench or cracked rock; a large targeted attack can destroy a city. Neither follows from arithmetic on a scouter number.

The current 400 was an authored Kame House snapshot, not a permanent ceiling or minimum. Both boys grow; Goshin uses flight and techniques and remains no less capable than Gohan by the user's direction. Do not force canon casualties or prescribe a lower-than-Nappa ceiling. See `canon/RESEARCH.md` for the evidence and limits.
